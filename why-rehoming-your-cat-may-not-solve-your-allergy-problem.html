Itchy eyes after the cat’s been on the bed? Hives that flare up indoors, but ease outdoors? A runny nose that just won’t go away? For a lot of cat owners, these symptoms raise the question: do I need to rehome my cat?

It’s an anxious question to sit with. But rehoming isn’t necessarily where this has to end up.

Before you make that difficult choice, it’s worth understanding how cat allergies actually work, where the allergens come from, and all the other options that new research now offers.

What's Actually Causing The Reaction

Cat allergies are commonly attributed to fur, but fur itself isn't the primary trigger. The main allergen involved is a protein called Fel d 1, which is produced in a cat's saliva and sebaceous glands.

During grooming, Fel d 1 transfers onto the coat, and from there, it doesn't stay put. It moves onto bedding, furniture, carpet, and clothing throughout the home.

Understanding this matters, because it means the allergic reaction isn't really to "the cat" themselves. You’re reacting to a specific protein that the cat continually produces and distributes.

Most Owners Don’t Realize: Cat Gone ≠ Allergen Gone

This is the part that often gets missed. Fel d 1 is a durable allergen.

Studies on indoor allergen persistence have found that cat allergens can remain detectable in household dust, upholstery, and carpet for months after a cat has been removed from a home, in some cases longer.

That means rehoming doesn't function as an immediate off switch. A household can go through the emotional and practical difficulty of giving up a cat, and still be living with reactive allergen levels, simply from what's already accumulated in soft surfaces and dust.

A More Useful Question Than "Keep Or Rehome?"

Given that allergen persistence, the black and white decision of keeping vs. rehoming the cat leaves out a crucial step.

A more useful question is: have I tried reducing allergen exposure first?

In practice, that usually means a layered approach rather than one single fix:

  • Frequent washing of bedding and soft furnishings
  • Keeping the cat out of the bedroom specifically
  • HEPA air filtration in high-exposure rooms
  • Medical allergy management (antihistamines, immunotherapy, or a specialist's guidance)
  • Directly reducing the allergen at its source and in the environment

Most owners have tried some combination of the first four.

Far fewer have addressed the fifth, largely because it's a newer category of intervention.

Two Ways To Fight Cat Allergens: Cat And Home

Because Fel d 1 originates with the cat and then spreads through the home, there are 2 different ways to intercept its spread.

You can lessen the allergens your cat produces in the first place, OR neutralize what is already lingering in your home.

Cat side: Pacagen's Cat Allergen Reducing Supplement is a powder mixed into a cat's food and is designed to target Fel d 1 in the cat's saliva, aiming to reduce the amount transferred onto the coat during grooming, before it has a chance to spread.

If you feel that your cat allergies flare up more in the soft surfaces in your home even after you remove your cat from the room, you can target that instead with a neutralizing product.

Home side: Pacagen's Cat Allergen Neutralizing Spray is designed to neutralize Fel d 1 that has already settled into bedding, furniture, and other household surfaces, addressing the allergen load that's already accumulated in the environment.

The products provide two different options to close the loop between where the allergen starts and where it ends up.

The Takeaway

Rehoming is a significant decision, and allergy symptoms are a legitimate reason to consider it. But cat allergens don't disappear the moment the cat does.

Understanding where Fel d 1 comes from, how it spreads, and how exposure can actually be reduced gives owners more complete information before deciding on that next step, rather than treating rehoming as a solution at all.
